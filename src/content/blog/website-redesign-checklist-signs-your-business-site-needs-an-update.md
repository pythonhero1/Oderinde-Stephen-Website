A website does not need a redesign simply because it is a few years old. Age can be a clue, but the stronger reasons are connected to business performance: visitors cannot understand the offer, mobile users struggle, updates are difficult or the website no longer represents the company accurately.

This checklist helps separate cosmetic preferences from problems that justify a redesign.

## 1. The Website No Longer Reflects the Business

Businesses evolve. Services change, teams grow, markets expand and positioning becomes clearer. If the website describes an earlier version of the company, it creates the wrong expectation before a conversation begins.

## 2. Visitors Cannot Understand the Offer Quickly

The homepage should help a suitable visitor recognise what the business provides, who it serves and what action to take next. Vague slogans force visitors to work too hard to decide whether the site is relevant.

## 3. Mobile Use Feels Like a Compromise

Responsive design is not simply shrinking a desktop layout. Check whether headings wrap cleanly, buttons are easy to tap, forms fit the screen, images remain meaningful and content appears in a sensible order.

## 4. The Website Is Noticeably Slow

Large images, unnecessary scripts, weak hosting and bloated plugins can make a website feel unresponsive. Slow loading affects confidence, search performance and conversions—especially on mobile connections.

## 5. Navigation Has Grown Without a Plan

Websites often become confusing gradually. New services are added wherever they fit, old pages remain and the navigation expands until visitors have too many choices.

A redesign should begin with a content audit and clearer information architecture, not only a new visual style.

## 6. Important Pages Have No Clear Next Step

Every page should have a purpose. A service page may lead to an enquiry, a case study to a related service and an article to deeper guidance. When pages end without a relevant action, interested visitors are left to navigate alone.

## 7. The Website Does Not Provide Enough Proof

Claims such as “high quality,” “trusted” and “professional” are not evidence. Prospective customers may need case studies, testimonials, reviews, accreditations, process details or real project examples before they are ready to enquire.

## 8. Content Updates Are Difficult or Risky

The business should not need a developer to correct a phone number or publish an ordinary article. If routine editing breaks layouts or exposes complex builder controls, the backend needs a better structure.

Reusable components and clearly defined content fields make future updates safer.

## 9. The Visual System Is Inconsistent

Inconsistent colours, buttons, spacing, images and typography make a site feel less trustworthy. This usually happens when pages are added by different people over several years.

A redesign can establish a coherent design system so new sections remain recognisably part of the same brand.

## 10. Enquiries Are Poorly Qualified

Low-quality enquiries are not always a marketing problem. The website may attract the wrong audience or fail to explain scope, process and pricing expectations.

Review the wording and form fields. Better qualification can reduce unsuitable enquiries while helping good prospects provide useful information.

## 11. The Website Depends on Outdated Technology

Unsupported themes, abandoned plugins and old platform versions create security and maintenance risks. Sometimes these issues can be repaired. In other cases, the technical foundation is so limiting that rebuilding becomes safer.

## 12. The Site Cannot Support the Next Business Goal

The clearest redesign trigger may be what the company needs next: a new market, online payments, multilingual content, a CRM connection or a client area.

If the current system cannot support that direction without fragile workarounds, a redesign can create a more appropriate foundation.

## Redesign, Improve or Rebuild?

Not every issue requires starting again. Focused improvements may be enough when the platform is sound and the problems are limited. A broader redesign is appropriate when the visual system, structure and customer journey all need work. A full rebuild makes sense when the technology cannot support the required result.

Review analytics, search visibility, forms, high-performing pages, internal links and existing content before changing the structure. Protect useful URLs and plan redirects where addresses must change.

A redesign should not exchange known business value for a cleaner appearance. Identify the real problems, prioritise them by business impact and define what success should look like after launch.
